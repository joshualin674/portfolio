@ Bits of Good

I redesigned high-impact fixes for Bagel Rescue and AARF, with each intervention reducing manual work, preventing mistakes, and helping nonprofit admins move with confidence.

overview

Maintaining and improving BOG's deployed nonprofits

After deploying these nonprofits, Bits of Good still finds moments within the nonprofits' software where small interface changes could unlock great operational value with fewer workarounds, faster edits, and clearer communication.

organization

Bagel Rescue

Bagel Rescue needed improvement to their admin control over volunteers, routes, and delivery locations to greatly reduce friction in doing such tasks.

01

Volunteer management: make editing seamless

Problem — admins do not have a streamlined way to delete and edit volunteers without leaving their main flow. This causes friction in daily tasks, slowing down their coordination, and thus reducing impact.

Fix no. 1 — added an options entry point with edit and deletion functionality, making both easy to access from one singular place.

Fix no. 2 — in addition to a pop-up modal, editing is also accessible through the volunteer information modal — another route to the task when viewing a volunteer's information.

Impact — reduced the path to editing and deletion by 40% in timed trials, giving admins a faster way to manage volunteers without leaving their primary workflow.

02

Route management: edit the system without rebuilding it

Problem — admins do not have a streamlined way to edit routes; it requires navigating away from the main flow, creating friction in day-to-day operations.

Fix — introduced the ability to edit a route from a modal, which takes you to the edit route page, where you can then edit locations.

Impact — removed the need to delete and recreate routes, reducing route modification time by approximately 40% and preserving operational context.

organization

AARF

Atlanta Animal Rescue Friends needed more effective volunteer operations across timesheets, emails, and ID records so staff could manage people faster and with greater ease.

01

Timesheet tracker: moving paperwork into a scan-friendly system

Problem — AARF currently uses a piece of paper and manually enters the information into an Excel sheet — slow, time-consuming manual entry.

Fix no. 1 — transitioned it into a mobile-first volunteer timesheet, similar in format to a Google Form — on-the-go and intuitive. The admin side features blocks that populate automatically as timesheets are submitted, plus search for finding a specific timesheet.

Fix no. 2 — also designed a desktop interface for users who prefer entering or reviewing timesheets on desktop.

Impact — made hour review easier on the go while keeping desktop admins in context — sped up timesheet organization by 65% versus manual entry.

02

Volunteer email: reminders that are consistent and clear

Problem — volunteers were not getting consistent reminders about upcoming shifts, resulting in missed shifts and manual weekly reminder emails.

Fix — created an email that is easy to automate and scan: preset text with interchangeable volunteer name and shift details, with sectioned details so the most important information is delivered quickly and at a glance.

Impact — a 15% attendance improvement through automating the message and making it clear and quickly scannable.

03

Volunteer ID deletion: an internal way to delete

Problem — admins lack an internal way to delete Volunteer IDs, so outdated or incorrect IDs remain in the system for weeks or months until a BoG developer can remove them.

Fix no. 1 — added a deletion icon to each ID in the list for direct deletion — red, to keep it distinct from editing.

Fix no. 2 — the edit ID modal also carries deletion for when they're already in an editing context.

Impact — reduced record-management friction by making ID deletion possible directly in the software, not through BoG.

takeaways

What this maintenance work taught me

Strong maintenance design is not about adding more UI. It is about finding the smallest point of leverage, clarifying the system around it, and helping users trust the next action.

Communication keeps sprints moving

Clarify confusing tickets early so design and engineering solve the same problem.

Mental models reduce overwhelm

Group dense information into categories users already expect.

Existing flows are assets

New features feel easier when they extend familiar patterns.

~ georgia tech oit ~

Center for Teaching & Learning

I redesigned CTL pages and blog experiences inside OIT's design system, turning dense information into clearer reading paths for students, faculty, and site maintainers.

overview

Modernization without starting over

The work focused on translating dense CTL content into a more readable system while preserving existing information, fitting OIT's component library, and staying realistic for Drupal and WordPress implementation.

project area

CTL site refresh

CTL's existing pages were content-heavy and visually outdated. The redesign used OIT's system to create stronger hierarchy, clearer section separation, and a structure users could scan faster.

01

Hierarchy: guide the eye through the page

Goal — rank the page content so users could understand what mattered first.

Fix — used larger hero treatments, clearer section modules, and supporting blocks to create a more intentional reading path.

Impact — students and faculty can scan major CTL information without parsing a dense wall of content.

02

Modular separation: reduce cognitive load

Goal — preserve all existing content while making sections feel distinct.

Fix — used generous spacing and modular OIT blocks so users process one category at a time.

Impact — the page feels easier to navigate while remaining scalable for future site updates.

subproblem

CTL Blog

The blog needed to make recent posts more visible, keep category browsing and search usable, and protect image visibility inside the reading experience.

01

Recent posts: make freshness obvious

Goal — help users find the newest CTL updates before older content takes over the page.

Fix — featured the three newest posts with larger image-led cards, then moved older posts into compact scan-friendly rows.

Impact — recent information becomes the default focus, reducing the need to dig through older content.

02

Filter and search: support multiple discovery paths

Goal — keep topic filters and keyword search available without overwhelming the page.

Fix — placed category filtering and search at the top so visitors can browse by topic or jump directly to a known post.

Impact — students and faculty have two clear paths to relevant information: topic-based filtration or direct search.

03

Image handling: keep visuals fully visible

Goal — avoid awkward cropping or text obstruction on thumbnails in reading pages.

Fix — separated image areas from text containers so metadata and titles never compete with screenshots or header images.

Impact — blog imagery stays legible, polished, and consistent across listing and detail pages.

outcomes

Clearer pages, easier updates

The redesigned CTL layouts make key announcements, recent posts, and major sections easier to scan while aligning with Georgia Tech's broader OIT design language and reducing maintenance overhead.

takeaways

What this redesign reinforced

Good information architecture is visible: hierarchy, spacing, and implementation constraints all shape whether users can understand a page quickly.

Hierarchy comes first

The most important content needs the strongest visual path.

Systems scale the work

Design systems make repeated site updates faster and more consistent.

Constraints shape outcomes

Drupal and WordPress limits made simple, buildable patterns more valuable.

~ spitfire ~

[an app for hot takes]

A social concept designed around hot takes, friend-based trust, and playful interaction — helping people share what they think to explore differing opinions.

overview

A safer stage for strong opinions

The core tension: people want to express bold takes, but mainstream feeds make disagreement feel public, permanent, and hostile. Spitfire reframes opinion-sharing as playful conversation built on trust.

Problem

Echo chambers reinforce bias, while toxic comment culture makes users afraid to post strong opinions.

Question

How might we build empathy and self-expression in a low-stakes, engaging, and fun way?

Approach

Start with trusted friends, then extend that empathy outward to strangers and their perspectives.

research

Insights that shaped the product

Survey and interview responses pointed to the same pattern: people are open with friends, hesitant in public, and interested in lightweight ways to stay connected.

48 participants

Survey responses helped identify where people felt safe sharing opinions.

7 interviews

Moderator conversations clarified why public social posting feels risky.

Friend trust

Users were willing to discuss beliefs with friends when respect already existed.

Public hesitation

Strong opinions felt stressful on mainstream platforms because strangers escalate quickly.

Real-time connection

People liked lightweight updates from close friends they do not talk to every day.

iteration

From research goals to flows

We translated the insights into a flow that moves from onboarding and identity creation into friend conversations, daily prompts, community discovery, and profile expression.

User flow — mapped signup, avatar creation, home, profile, and community interactions around empathy-building goals.

Lo-fi prototype — used quick structure to test whether the concept made sense before visual polish.

final design

A playful product system for perspective-taking

The final app uses soft curves, bold graphics, familiar social mechanics, and clear spaces for friends and discovery — balancing fun with emotional safety.

01

Splash and signup: establish a bold, playful identity

Goal — make the product feel approachable before asking people to share strong opinions.

Choice — used soft curves, bold graphics, and a clear onboarding path to create a lighter emotional tone.

Outcome — the first impression frames debate as playful conversation, not conflict.

02

Avatar creation: make identity expressive, not exposing

Goal — let users personalize themselves while keeping the experience lighthearted.

Choice — added avatar creation as a playful extension of self-expression.

Outcome — users can show personality without relying only on real-world identity cues.

03

Home feed: borrow familiar scroll behavior for hot takes

Goal — make the core interaction instantly understandable and fun.

Choice — used a Reel/TikTok-style scroll for friend posts, reactions, comments, and daily prompts.

Outcome — the interface lowers learning friction and encourages recurring lightweight engagement.

04

Friends and discovery: bridge comfort with perspective

Goal — support trusted conversations while still exposing users to different opinions.

Choice — separated friend/group conversations from discovery, where other viewpoints can surface by topic.

Outcome — Spitfire creates a path from familiar empathy to broader perspective-taking.

05

Profile: let takes become a personal archive

Goal — give users a place to own, pin, and revisit what they have shared.

Choice — designed a profile for viewing and pinning favorite takes, visible when others explore a user.

Outcome — opinions become part of a lightweight identity system without overwhelming the main feed.

takeaways

What Spitfire taught me

This project showed how research, prototyping, and feedback can turn a broad social problem into a focused product story.

Prototype earlier

Mid-fi and hi-fi prototypes helped test interaction assumptions before the final visual system.

Interview deeply

Interviews gave the team language for the emotional risk behind posting opinions.

Test blind spots

Feedback revealed which interactions felt intuitive only to the design team.
